## BEAT 1 — HOOK (0:00)
Marcus needed a car. Not wanted. *Needed.*
His old one had died on the highway, and his job was forty minutes away.

## BEAT 2 (0:08)
So Marcus walked into a dealership, credit score 720, and twenty-two thousand dollars sitting in savings.
The dealer smiled. Ran the numbers. And told him something that *sounded smart.*

## BEAT 3 (0:16)
"Don't touch your savings. Finance it. You're approved at 6.9% for 72 months."
Marcus thought about it. His savings, safe. His car, new. It sounded perfect.

## BEAT 4 (0:24)
So he signed. A thirty-five-thousand-dollar truck, financed at 6.9%, for six long years.
And that's where the story usually ends. But today, we run the numbers.

## BEAT 5 (0:32)
Here's the seven-year math — because that's the real lifetime of this decision.
Total price of the truck: thirty-five thousand dollars.
But over 72 months at 6.9%, Marcus pays interest on every single one of them.

## BEAT 6 (0:40)
By the time the loan is done, he's paid more than eight thousand dollars — just in interest.
Eight thousand. That's a second car. That's two years of groceries.

## BEAT 7 (0:48)
And that's only the part with the interest rate on it.
Because cars don't hold value. That brand-new truck loses about a fifth of its value the second it leaves the lot.

## BEAT 8 (0:56)
Year one, it's worth twenty-eight thousand. Year three, twenty-one.
Meanwhile, the loan balance barely moves in the early years. You pay interest first.

## BEAT 9 (1:04)
So for most of the loan, Marcus owes *more* than the truck is worth.
That's called being underwater. And it feels exactly like it sounds.

## BEAT 10 (1:12)
Now let's do the cash side. Same truck. Thirty-five thousand dollars, paid in full.
Marcus keeps the dealer's paperwork, but he keeps something else too — the interest.

## BEAT 11 (1:20)
Because that twenty-two thousand in savings? It was earning him money.
Even a boring savings account pays something. And he kept earning on it, every single year.

## BEAT 12 (1:28)
Over seven years, that difference compounds.
The financed truck costs Marcus about forty-seven thousand all-in.
The cash truck? Thirty-five thousand. Plus everything his savings kept growing.

## BEAT 13 (1:36)
The gap: more than twelve thousand dollars. For the same truck. Same roads. Same commute.
The only difference is who got to keep the money.

## BEAT 14 (1:44)
But wait — financing isn't always wrong. This is the part nobody tells you.
If the rate is low, and the money can grow faster than the loan costs, financing can win.

## BEAT 15 (1:52)
Marcus's rate was 6.9%. His savings were earning a fraction of that.
He was paying six-point-nine to keep money that made him almost nothing. That math loses every time.

## BEAT 16 (2:00)
So here's the rule. Before you finance anything, ask one question:
Can my money make more than this loan costs? If not — pay cash, or wait.

## BEAT 17 (2:08)
And the second rule? Never finance a thing that loses value.
Financing a home, an education, a business — that can build wealth. A car is not an investment. It's a tool.

## BEAT 18 (2:16)
Marcus's mistake wasn't buying the truck. It was never seeing the real number.
The sticker said thirty-five thousand. The real number was forty-seven.

## BEAT 19 (2:24)
One spreadsheet, ten minutes, and he'd have seen the whole thing.
That's the entire trick to money. Not being smarter. Just running the numbers.

## BEAT 20 — CLOSE (2:32)
So before you sign the next loan, ask your dealer what the truck *really* costs — over seven years.
If he hesitates, you already know the answer.

## BEAT 21 (2:40)
Run the seven-year math. Keep your interest. And let the compound work for you — not against you.

## BEAT 22 (2:48)
Because the best financial decision you ever make isn't about getting more.
It's about keeping what you already have.
